📘 What is a Rheostat?

A rheostat is a variable resistor designed to control electric current in a circuit without interrupting the flow. The name was coined in the 1840s by Sir Charles Wheatstone from the Greek words rheos (stream/flow) and -stat (regulating device).

Unlike tiny carbon-film potentiometers used on circuit boards, rheostats are typically built with heavy-gauge resistive wire (such as Nichrome or Constantan) wound around a heat-resistant ceramic core so they can safely dissipate significant heat.

R = ρ · (L / A)

Resistance (R) increases proportionally with the active wire length (L). Moving the sliding wiper simply changes how much coiled wire the current must travel through!

🛠️ Anatomy of a Sliding Rheostat

1. Ceramic Core

An electrical insulator that supports the coils and withstands high temperatures.

2. Resistance Wire

High-resistivity alloy (like Nichrome) tightly wound in insulated turns.

3. Sliding Wiper

A spring-loaded metal contact that taps into a specific turn along the coil.

4. 3 Terminals (A, B, C)

Two fixed ends (A, B) plus one wiper terminal (C). Only two are wired as a rheostat.

🔀 Rheostat vs. Potentiometer: What's the Difference?

Physically, a 3-terminal sliding or rotary component can often act as either one! The difference is how it is wired and what electrical quantity it controls.

🌍 Common Types & Real-World Uses

📏 Linear (Sliding)

The classic laboratory tube rheostat simulated above. Great for physics experiments, load banks, and calibrating instruments.

🎛️ Rotary (Wire-Wound)

Shaped like a circular arc with a turning knob. Compact and commonly used in older dashboard dimmers and industrial motor controls.

⚙️ Modern Replacement

Because rheostats waste energy as heat (P = I²R), modern dimmers and motor drives mostly use efficient PWM (Pulse-Width Modulation) or TRIACs instead.
